# County communities: research manifest

This note records the curated static communities attached to the 867 and 1066
bookmark counties. It is an editorial gameplay model, not a population estimate:
the arrow preserves authored display order, the first pair is the principal county
identity, and later pairs make other grounded local starts available. No percentage,
conversion, migration, unrest, or demographic change is implied.

The detailed manifest contains 182 bookmark-county records: 72 in 867 and 110 in
1066, spanning 25 regional groupings. County ids remain stable even when the principal
identity changes between bookmarks.

## Principal culture seeding

The culture table also includes principal identities that do not need a multi-community
county record. Each has its own personal-name and settlement-fragment pools, dynasty
rule, cultural affinity, technology tradition, and deterministic portrait profile.

| Bookmark | Principal culture | Seeded core |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Both | Occitan | Toulouse, Albi, Cahors, Rodez, Narbonne, Béziers, Carcassonne, Arles, Marseille, Avignon, Nice |
| Both | Lombard | Benevento, Capua, Salerno |
| Both | Finnic | Tallinn, Tartu, Turku, Häme, Savo, Kexholm |
| 867 | Sámi | Norrland; Hålogaland remains principally Norse and offers a paired Sámi start |
| 867 | Khazar | Atil, Sarkel, Tmutarakan, Samandar; 1066 keeps only an authored remnant at Atil |
| 1066 | Norman | Rouen, Caen, Évreux, Bayeux, plus a secondary Norman start at Robert Guiscard's Foggia capital |
| 1066 | Rus | The Kievan, Novgorodian, Chernigovan, Pereyaslavlian, Polotsk, and Turov realm counties |

Coptic and Syriac are deliberately not made principal in these bookmarks. Their paired
communities represent established Christian identities without inventing Coptic/Shia,
Syriac/Sunni, or Arabic/Eastern matchmaking combinations. `norse_pagan` remains the
closest available faith vocabulary for Sámi traditional religion; this is a stated
abstraction, not a claim that the two religions were identical.

## Evidence and editorial use

- The Iberian secondary Catholic identity represents Mozarab communities documented
  throughout al-Andalus, including Garb al-Andalus (Coimbra, Santarém, Silves, Lisbon),
  the Upper March (Ebro valley), and southern Andalusia.
- In 1066 al-Andalus, the Zirid Taifa of Granada and Aftasid Taifa of Badajoz feature
  Berber communities alongside the Andalusi and Mozarab populations.
- Basque (`basque`) is modeled as an independent ethnolinguistic culture centered in
  Pamplona (the Kingdom of Navarre), with coexisting Basque and Romance communities
  across Álava, Logroño (La Rioja), northern Aragon, and Bayonne (Gascony).
  See the review of *The Formation of al-Andalus, Part 1:
  History and Society* in [The Medieval Review](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/16918).
- The east-Breton reversal between 867 and 1066 follows the documented growth of
  Frankish political and cultural influence without erasing Brythonic communities.
  See [“From invasion to conquest: Brittany and its history, 919–1066”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/08AE7653E81EE433233ACDBA655EC955/9781108486514c5_180-230.pdf/from-invasion-to-conquest-brittany-and-its-history-919-1066.pdf).
- Southern Scotland is represented with distinct Brythonic, Gaelic, and English
  communities rather than collapsing the region into one tag. See
  [*Gaelic and the Highland Council*](https://www.highland.gov.uk/membersintranet/download/downloads/id/242/gaelic_and_the_highland_council.pdf).
- Eastern Anatolia keeps Armenian settlement visible under changing Greek and Turkic
  political contexts. See the regional study in
  [*MOM Éditions*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/mom_0766-0510_1993_sem_21_1_1809).
- The Balkan pairs represent Greek–Slavic coexistence and bookmark-local principal
  changes, not homogeneous ethnic blocks. See
  [“Slav Christianities, 800–1100”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-christianity/slav-christianities-8001100/7FC760653F6016118C3C32B4A93B0D22).
- The Ashkenazi/Jewish records are restricted to communities attested by or before
  1066: Metz (893), Regensburg (981), Mainz (around 1000), Cologne (1010/12), and
  Worms (1034) appear in the comparative chronology of the
  [ShUM nomination dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/document/185389); the
  [YIVO Encyclopedia's Ashkenaz overview](https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article/2373)
  places early Ashkenazic centers in Mainz, Worms, and northern France; and Troyes is
  attested from the tenth century in the
  [Jewish Encyclopedia](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/14528-troyes).
  Trier is separately attested in 1066 in the
  [Jewish Encyclopedia](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/14502-treves).
  Speyer is not backdated: its community was founded in 1084. `paired` preserves the
  already combined Ashkenazi/Jewish identity instead of generating Catholic or
  German/Frankish-Jewish recombinations.
- Ninth-century Egyptian sources distinguish an indigenous Coptic population while
  also documenting increasing Arab settlement and institutional Arabic. See Oxford's
  studies of [Egyptian identities](https://academic.oup.com/book/34785/chapter-abstract/297602178)
  and [early Islamic Egyptian elites](https://academic.oup.com/book/38915/chapter/338088800).
  The paired secondary identity therefore remains visible under both Abbasid Sunni and
  Fatimid Shia principal baselines.
- Edessa retained a substantial Christian population for centuries after the Muslim
  conquest, while Syriac Christian identity had an established center there. See
  [Encyclopaedia Iranica on Edessa](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/edessa/)
  and the survey of [Syriac church identity](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/14/9/1179).
  Mosul, Amida, and Edessa are a restrained northern-Mesopotamian seed rather than an
  assertion that every Syriac-speaking county was homogeneous.
- Medieval archaeology places Finnic and Baltic peoples across northeastern Europe and
  records close interaction with Rus culture; this supports distinct Finnic cores plus
  paired communities around Novgorod and Beloozero. See the Russian Academy of Sciences'
  [medieval Finno-Ugric and Baltic survey](https://archaeolog.ru/el-bib/el-cat/el-series/arch-sssr/archaeologyussr_09).
- The northern Sámi seed is intentionally sparse. The continuing Sámi cultural landscape
  and reindeer-based lifeways of northern Sweden are documented by UNESCO's
  [Laponian Area](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/774/).
- Norman is restricted to eleventh-century Normandy and a small southern-Italian
  foothold. Scholarship treats the principality as possessing a distinct identity by
  the late tenth and eleventh centuries; see the
  [Early Medieval Europe study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/emed.12106)
  and the review of [Norman identity's evolution](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/16017).
- The Beneventan principal culture follows the documented Lombard duchy/principality and
  its ninth-century court culture. See
  [Firenze University Press](https://books.fupress.com/chapter/framing-the-beneventan-aristocracy-in-the-first-half-of-the-ninth-century/16300).
- Occitan is limited to the Toulouse, Narbonne, and Provençal core. Eleventh-century
  charters contain Occitan vernacular, while scholarship treats Toulouse, Albi,
  Rouergue, Carcassonne, and the Mediterranean corridor as a connected southern field;
  see [Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bec_0373-6237_1997_num_155_1_450862)
  and [The Medieval Review](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/15845).
- Rus is a 1066 principal identity, while the more contested 867 ethnogenesis remains
  a paired option in Novgorod, Ladoga, and Kiev. The distinction reflects the mixed
  Slavo-Finno-Scandinavian frontier discussed in the
  [Institute of Ethnology and Anthropology](https://eo.iea.ras.ru/en/article/22248/).
- Khazar is principal only in the 867 khaganate. Its Jewish elite and the disputed extent
  of conversion are represented by paired Jewish and Tengri identities at Atil instead
  of making every Khazar Jewish; see Cambridge's
  [study of Khazaria's monotheisation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/monotheisation-of-ponticcaspian-eurasia/monotheisation-of-khazaria/313AF2059457907FCFDE5A5CB1797910).

The remaining selections use the same restrained rule: include a second identity only
where it adds a recognizable local start within the game's existing culture and faith
vocabulary, and keep the first pair aligned with the bookmark's simulation baseline.

## Remaining abstractions

A distinct Sámi faith and narrower regional Finnic cultures remain possible future
refinements. Tmutarakan's 1066 political ownership also remains outside this culture
pass; it keeps the bookmark's Cuman/Turkic baseline rather than being used as evidence
for a larger surviving Khazar block.
